A month into the fall semester, I haven't taught a class. That is what a research leave looks like from the outside: an absence. From the inside it is a deadline. I'm building a course called *Exploring Generative AI in Creative Workflows*. It is first taught on 11 January, its description is now in the course catalogue, and students are about to decide from one paragraph whether to take it.

If you've read this blog before, you know it as a place where I read film and television through psychoanalysis. That hasn't stopped. It has picked up a second subject, and I want to work on it here in the open. I should say what I mean by that, because "blogging the process" usually means announcing conclusions once they are safe. What I intend is narrower and, I hope, more useful: each post takes one decision I actually had to make in building this course and shows the reasoning, including the decisions I got wrong the first time. There are already several.

The obvious version of this course teaches the tools. Students learn Firefly, learn a prompt syntax, make something, and leave employable. I understand why that course is attractive, and I'm not going to teach it. The tools I would name in a syllabus written this September will have changed by January, and some of them won't exist. A course built on a tool list is out of date before its first meeting.

The alternative usually on offer fails in a different way: a course *about* AI, taught at arm's length, all critique and no making. My students are in media production programs. They are going to use these tools whether or not anyone teaches them well, and a course that only theorises what they will do on Monday isn't serving them either.

So the design problem is holding both, and that is harder than it sounds. The course has to teach capabilities that outlast the products that deliver them today — generating, editing, extending, restoring, translating, directing — and it has to put students' hands on those products anyway. It has to require documented AI use as assessed work while forbidding AI as a substitute for the work itself: two sentences that sit side by side in a policy paragraph I am still writing.

In October I take the same question to India. I'll be asking academics and media practitioners how AI is actually being used in production, from preproduction through post, and what they would want a graduate to walk in already able to do. That is the other half of what this blog will follow.

Not a record of a course being taught, then, but of one being built: the part that usually happens out of sight and then gets reported as though it were obvious.
